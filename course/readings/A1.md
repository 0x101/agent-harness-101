## In one paragraph

Anthropic's engineers report that the teams who built the most successful agents did it with simple patterns they could combine, not with frameworks. The post separates two kinds of system. In a **workflow**, your code decides the path and the model fills in steps. In an **agent**, the model decides what to do next. It names five workflow patterns, describes the agent as a model using tools in a loop, and argues that you should add complexity only when you can show it helps.

## How to read it for this course

About 15 minutes. It is a short post with no math.

- **Read closely:** [What are agents?](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#what-are-agents), [When (and when not) to use agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#when-and-when-not-to-use-agents), [Agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#agents), the [Summary](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#summary), and [Appendix 2](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#appendix-2-prompt-engineering-your-tools).
- **Skim for the names:** the five workflow patterns, from prompt chaining to evaluator-optimizer. You will meet several of them again as you build.
- **Skip:** the list of frameworks, and the customer support half of Appendix 1.
- **S01, What a harness is.** Read it with `harness/agent.py` open. For each pattern and each piece of advice, ask whether the starter agent has it.

A note at the top of the post says much of the tooling it mentions has changed since December 2024. The patterns are what you are reading it for, and they have not.

## What it shows

This is an experience report. Nothing in it is measured, so treat each point as what one team saw.

- **Simple beat complex in the field.** The authors worked with "dozens of teams" and found the successful ones were not using complex frameworks ([introduction](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)).
- **An agent is a small program.** It is a model using tools in a loop, taking feedback from its environment at each step, with a stopping condition such as a maximum number of iterations ([Agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#agents)).
- **Tools deserve as much care as prompts.** While building an agent for a coding benchmark, the authors spent more time on tools than on the overall prompt. One example: the model made mistakes with relative file paths after changing directory, and requiring absolute paths ended them ([Appendix 2](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents#appendix-2-prompt-engineering-your-tools)).

## Where it is weak

- It gives no numbers. That simple designs work better is a claim from experience, and the post does not say how many teams, which tasks, or how success was judged.
- It is written by a model vendor about its own customers.
- It tells you to measure before adding complexity but not how. That gap is what this course's eval set is for, and P5 in S09 takes up the question of how to compare agents fairly.

## What it means for your harness

The starter agent is the post's "agent" in its barest form: one tool, one loop, and a step cap as its only stopping condition. Most of what the post recommends beyond that is missing on purpose, and each later module adds a piece.

The line to carry through the whole course is the one about complexity. Most sessions from here on add something to your harness and end with a measurement. If a change does not show up in the numbers, the post's advice is to take it out.

Appendix 2 is the seed of module M1. Its claim that small changes to a tool's interface remove whole classes of mistakes is tested with numbers by P1 in S04.
