## In one paragraph

Researchers at Princeton argue that agent benchmarks reward the wrong thing. Accuracy can be bought by calling the model again, so a comparison that ignores cost says little. They plot both on a **Pareto curve**, the agents no other agent beats on cost and accuracy at once, and simple baselines come out ahead of elaborate coding agents. They also show that benchmarks without held-out tasks invite **overfitting**, where an agent fits the test instead of the job, and that many published results do not reproduce.

## What it shows

- **Simple baselines match complex agents for far less.** On HumanEval's 164 problems, each agent run five times, a warming baseline (retrying with a rising temperature) showed no significant accuracy difference from the best agent architecture. Reflexion and LDB cost over 50% more than warming, LATS over 50 times more ([2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S2.SS3)). A measurement.
- **Benchmarks allow shortcuts.** Of 17 agent benchmarks surveyed, 7 have no hold-out set and no stated plan for one ([section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S5)). STeP, the top WebArena agent at 35.8%, hardcodes policies for the benchmark's tasks ([5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S5.SS1)). A survey and a case study.
- **Cost makes repeats rare.** At SWE-agent's cap of USD 4 per task, one run over SWE-bench's 2,000-plus tasks could cost over USD 8,000, so agent results rarely have error bars. Many reported scores beat the best of the authors' five reproduction runs ([section 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S6)). A reproduction study.

## Where it is weak

- The cost result is on HumanEval, single coding problems with example tests given. The authors allow that System 2 methods may help on harder tasks such as SWE-bench ([2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S2.SS3)), which are closer to your repository fixes.
- Their cheap baselines retry until the example tests pass. Your harness has no such check until module M6.
- Prices date from April and May 2024 ([A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#A1.SS1), [B.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#A2.SS1)), a limitation the authors name ([Appendix G](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#A7)). Their stable-ratio argument rests on nearly all cost being GPT-4 calls, so it does not hold across models.
- They compare whole agent designs, not parts such as a tool set.

## What it means for your harness

From S09 on, every measurement reports tokens next to pass rate. A change earns its place only if it raises the pass rate by more than noise (one flip of 12 tasks is 8 points), or keeps it and costs less. In S09, if the full tool set only ties bash plus truncation, the cheaper arm wins.

Section 4 asks for input and output tokens beside dollars, so cost can be recomputed. Your runner prints both per task, but `history.tsv` keeps only their sum. For S31, take the split from the per-run JSONL files in `evals/results/`.

Section 5 bears on S32: tasks you write there, untuned, are your nearest thing to a held-out set.

## In their words

> "Accuracy alone cannot identify progress because it can be improved by scientifically meaningless methods such as retrying."
>
> Source: [section 2.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S2.SS3)

> "In turn, downstream evaluations of agents should include input/output token counts in addition to dollar costs, so that anyone looking at the evaluation in the future can instantly recalculate the cost using current prices."
>
> Source: [section 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502#S4)
